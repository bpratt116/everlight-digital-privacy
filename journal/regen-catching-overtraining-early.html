Overtraining: how to catch it before it derails your season

July 2026

Every training plan worth following is going to make you tired sometimes. That's not a bug — pushing your body past its current comfort zone and then letting it adapt is the whole mechanism behind getting fitter. The problem is that "productively tired" and "sliding into overtraining" feel almost identical in the moment, and the only reliable way to tell them apart is to watch what happens over weeks, not days.

Overload versus overtraining

Healthy training overload looks like this: a hard week or a hard block leaves you fatigued, your numbers dip a bit, and then with a lighter day or two, you bounce back — often stronger than before. That bounce-back is the entire point. It's called supercompensation for a reason: the dip is temporary and the rebound overshoots your previous baseline.

Overtraining, or the earlier and more common version called overreaching, is what happens when the hard stretches keep stacking without enough of that lighter time in between. The dip stops resolving. You start the next hard session from a hole instead of from a rebound, and each subsequent session digs a little deeper. It's a gradual erosion, not a single bad day — which is exactly why it's so easy to miss while it's happening.

The early warning signs

The signs are there well before a season-ending injury or a burnout. Heart rate variability trending down over multiple weeks, not just a single off morning, is one of the earliest and most reliable. A resting heart rate that's crept a few beats above your normal baseline and stays there is another. Sleep that's gotten shorter or more restless, even though nothing else in life changed, often shows up alongside it.

Then there are the signs you'd notice without any data at all, if you were paying attention: performance that plateaus or quietly regresses despite training harder, workouts that used to feel manageable now feeling disproportionately brutal, and a flatness or lack of motivation that isn't really about willpower — it's your body pulling the plug on drive because it's out of resources to spend.

Why it's so easy to miss

The hard part isn't the science — it's the psychology. Most people chasing a goal have internalized the idea that if a workout feels hard, that's proof it's working. So the exact signals that should prompt backing off — persistent fatigue, a flat mood, a session that felt worse than it should have — get reinterpreted as "I just need to push through" or "I'm being soft." By the time it's undeniable, the hole is usually deep enough that the fix isn't a rest day, it's a rest week or two, or worse, an actual injury that forces the issue.

This is also why single-day snapshots aren't enough to catch it. One rough morning is just a rough morning — it happens constantly in healthy training and means nothing on its own. What actually catches overtraining early is watching the trend across two, three, four weeks and noticing when the normal up-and-down stops recovering and starts sliding.
