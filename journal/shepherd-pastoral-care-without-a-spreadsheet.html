Keeping track of pastoral care without a spreadsheet

July 2026

Ask a pastor how they keep track of who needs care, and a surprising number will tell you the truth: a spreadsheet, a notebook, and their memory. For a small congregation, that's genuinely enough — you can hold a hundred names and stories in your head and know exactly who's in the hospital and who you haven't seen in a while. The trouble starts when the congregation grows past what one memory can hold, and care quietly starts running on whoever happens to come to mind.

What a spreadsheet can't do

A spreadsheet is a fine list of people. It's a poor record of care, because care isn't a column — it's a history. Who visited Margaret after her surgery, and what did they learn? Was the prayer request from three weeks ago ever followed up on? Which families haven't had any contact from the church in months — not because anyone decided to neglect them, but because they're quiet and never came up? A grid of rows can't answer those questions, so the answers live in individual people's heads, which means they're invisible to everyone else and gone the moment that person is out.

The second thing a spreadsheet can't do is handle sensitivity. Pastoral notes aren't all the same. Some are fine for the whole care team to see, some belong only to the pastor who wrote them, and some are genuinely confidential. A shared spreadsheet forces an impossible choice: either everything is visible to everyone with access, or the sensitive things get left out entirely and the record becomes incomplete. Neither is right for the work.

What's actually worth tracking

You don't need to track everything — trying to log a congregation's entire life is how a tool becomes a burden nobody maintains. A workable record needs only a few things. People and how they connect, so a family shows up as a family and context is there before a visit. Care history — a lightweight note of who reached out, when, and what came of it. Prayer requests, from ask to answer, so nothing raised on a Sunday is quietly forgotten by the next. And a way to see who's gone quiet, because the people who never ask for attention are exactly the ones a busy week overlooks.

The point of tracking any of this isn't administration for its own sake. It's so that care depends on a shared record instead of one person's memory — so a congregation is cared for consistently, not just when someone happens to think of it.

Where Shepherd comes in

Prayer requests move from ask to answer in a shared view the whole team can see.
