Building Shepherd: pastoral care software churches actually use

July 2026

A pastor at a mid-size church might carry, in their head, a rough map of two hundred people: who's in the hospital, whose marriage is strained, who just had a baby, who hasn't been seen in a month and might be quietly drifting away. That map used to live on index cards, then in a spreadsheet, then in a notes app, then nowhere in particular — a mix of memory, sticky notes, and hoping the right thing surfaces at the right time. This is the actual problem pastoral care software is supposed to solve, and most of it doesn't.

The problem: care is easy to lose track of

It's not that pastors don't care. It's that care work is inherently unstructured — someone mentions in passing that their dad is sick, someone else stops coming to the Sunday service they'd been at for years, a text comes in at 9pm about a hospital visit. None of that arrives as a task on a list. It arrives as a moment, and if there's no easy place to capture it, it gets forgotten, not out of neglect but because human memory isn't built to hold two hundred people's evolving situations at once. The pastors I've talked to don't need a productivity system. They need a place to jot down "check on the Andersons" in fifteen seconds and trust it'll come back to them later.

Why existing church management software fails small churches

Most church management software (ChMS) was built to solve a different problem: the church office's problem. It's optimized for tracking giving, managing membership rolls, scheduling volunteers, and running reports for the board. Those are real needs, but they're administrative needs, not pastoral ones. A pastor opening one of these tools to log a hospital visit is dropped into a system designed for someone else's job — dozens of fields, a UI built around forms and reports, and a mental model built around "records" instead of "people I'm walking alongside."

There's also a cost problem. Most ChMS platforms price per-member, which sounds reasonable until you realize it punishes the exact behavior you want to encourage: tracking care for more people. A small church with 150 members already has a tight budget, and per-member pricing for a care log makes covering fewer people look artificially cheaper. That's backwards. And because these platforms bundle a dozen modules a small church will never touch — giving portals, event registration, background-check integrations — the price tag reflects all of it, whether you use it or not.

How Shepherd is different

Shepherd starts from the pastor's actual workflow, not the office's. Its core unit isn't a membership record, it's a person and the thread of care around them: notes, prayer requests, milestones, and who on the team is responsible for following up. Adding a note takes seconds, from a phone, in the parking lot after a hospital visit. Notes can be shared with the care team, kept private to one elder, or marked confidential — because pastoral information isn't all the same sensitivity, and a tool that treats it as one undifferentiated blob either overshares or forces everything into total secrecy.

Shepherd also has flock assignments, so care isn't just one overloaded pastor's job — a "My People" view lets each elder or care-team member see exactly who they're responsible for, and nothing gets silently unassigned when the load needs to spread out. And pricing is one flat number per tier, not per member, so growing your care coverage never costs more per person you're actually caring for.

This is what "pastoral care software" should mean: not a records system, but a way to make sure the person who mentioned their dad was sick three weeks ago doesn't get lost between then and now. Small churches, especially, run on relationships more than systems — Shepherd's job is to make sure the system doesn't get in the way of that, and actually holds what the pastor's memory can't hold alone.

Church care, done well, isn't about better reporting. It's about not letting people fall out of view. That's the whole premise Shepherd was built on, and it's the reason I keep building it.
