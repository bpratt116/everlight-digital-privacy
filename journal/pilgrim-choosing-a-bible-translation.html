Choosing a Bible translation: a plain-English guide

July 2026

Open any Bible app and you'll hit a wall of abbreviations before you've read a word of scripture — ESV, NIV, NLT, NASB, KJV, CSB, and a dozen others. Nobody explains what they actually mean in practice, so people either pick whatever their church uses or whatever shows up first in the list. That's not a terrible way to choose, but it's worth understanding the actual differences, because they matter more for daily reading than most people realize.

The word-for-word vs. thought-for-thought spectrum

Every translation sits somewhere on a spectrum between two approaches. On one end is word-for-word translation, sometimes called formal equivalence — the translators try to preserve the original Hebrew or Greek sentence structure and word choice as closely as English allows. The King James Version, the English Standard Version, and the New American Standard Bible all lean heavily this direction. The upside is precision: you're getting something close to the actual grammar and phrasing of the original text. The downside is that biblical Hebrew and Greek don't map cleanly onto English, so these translations can read stiffly, with long sentences and word orders that take more effort to parse.

On the other end is thought-for-thought translation, or dynamic equivalence — the translators focus on capturing the meaning of a passage and then express it in natural, contemporary English, even if that means restructuring the sentence. The New Living Translation sits here, along with looser paraphrases. The upside is readability — it often reads like something written yesterday. The downside is that more interpretive choices get made on your behalf, since a single "meaning" sometimes flattens ambiguity that existed in the original.

Most of the translations people actually use day to day — the NIV, the CSB — sit in the middle. They aim for a balance: readable modern English that still tracks reasonably close to the original structure. This middle ground is why they're often recommended as a starting point.

Readability vs. precision, and why that tradeoff matters

This isn't just academic. A translation you find hard to read is a translation you'll read less. If a formal, word-for-word version leaves you re-reading sentences three times to understand them, that friction adds up over weeks and months, and it's often the quiet reason someone's daily reading habit doesn't survive past a few weeks. On the other hand, if precision matters to you — you're doing close study, comparing wording, or want to get as near to the original phrasing as English allows — a looser thought-for-thought version can smooth over distinctions worth noticing.

Neither end of the spectrum is "more accurate" in a simple sense. Word-for-word accuracy in structure isn't the same as accuracy in communicating meaning to a modern reader, and vice versa. They're solving different problems.

How to pick for your purpose

The honest answer is that the right translation depends on what you're doing with it. For close study — comparing specific word choices, working through a passage verse by verse with commentary — a word-for-word translation like the ESV or NASB gives you more to work with. For devotional reading, where the point is absorbing the flow and meaning of a passage during a quiet morning or evening, something more toward the readable end, like the NIV or CSB, tends to hold attention better. For someone brand new to reading scripture at all, or reading with kids, the NLT's plain contemporary English removes a real barrier that a denser translation would put in their way.

And it's completely fine — good, even — to use more than one. A lot of people read a devotional passage in something readable day to day, then switch to a more literal translation when they're digging into a specific verse or preparing to teach. There's no rule that says you owe loyalty to a single translation for life.
